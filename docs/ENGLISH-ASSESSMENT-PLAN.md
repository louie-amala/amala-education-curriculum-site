# The English Reading, Writing and Speaking Check — baseline and endline

_Planning doc. Nothing is built yet. The instrument measures the English Language Development
component of Learning Bridge+ (Cox's Bazar) — see `content-source/units/coxs-bazar-my-voice.yaml`._

## What this is

A pair of parallel forms — **Form A** taken in the first sessions of the component, **Form B** taken
in the last — that place a learner on a published proficiency ladder and show, defensibly, whether
they moved. It replaces nothing: the component's formative assessment stays exactly as
`coxs-bazar-my-voice.yaml` describes it. This sits alongside, to answer one question the growing My
Voice book cannot answer on its own — *did English improve, and by how much?*

Total sitting time is 35–45 minutes for the paper parts, plus a 6-minute one-to-one oral check.

## The cohort decides the shape

The programme summary says it plainly: learners are below B1, **many are not yet literate in any
language**, and My Voice is built as emergent literacy — "it assumes no prior reading in any
language and is delivered oral and visual first". The component targets A1−.

So an instrument reaching to B2 would spend most of its length above every learner in the room. The
design below inverts the usual weighting:

- **Thick** from below-A1 to A2 — sub-banded finely, because that is the whole measurement zone.
- **Thin** above it — one short reach booklet at B1, so an outlier learner is not ceilinged.
- **Nothing above B1.** B1 is the top of the ladder and the top of the instrument.

### Why the ladder stops at B1

B1 is the meaningful external threshold: it is the entry requirement for the Global Secondary
Diploma, so "reached B1" is a result that means something outside this programme. It is also
already above the ceiling of the English pathway itself — English for Impact runs 250 hours to
reach a strong A2+ and only aspires toward B1.

Measuring B2 would cost roughly a third of the paper time on a band that will return no data, and
it needs a marker who can reliably separate B1 from B2 in free writing — which we cannot assume
here. A band nobody on the ground can apply is worse than no band, because it produces numbers that
look like measurement.

A learner who tops out the B1 booklet is recorded as **"above the range of this instrument"** and
referred for a separate placement. That is an enrolment finding as much as a score: a learner
genuinely above B1 is misplaced in an emergent-literacy course, and the check surfacing it is
useful in itself.

### Why "below A1" needs its own scale

The CEFR assumes literacy even at A1 — its A1 reading descriptor is already "familiar names, words
and very simple sentences… on notices and posters". A learner who is learning to read *at all* sits
underneath the framework, not at the bottom of it. Reporting them as "A1, not achieved" tells you
nothing and, over a 50-hour course, will show no movement at all.

The Council of Europe's **LASLLIAM** guide (2022) fills exactly this gap: four-level can-do scales
running up to A1, written for non- and low-literate adult migrants and refugees with little or no
prior schooling. We borrow its shape for the bottom of our ladder — not its full descriptor set, but
the principle that the pre-A1 span is several measurable steps, not one.

## The ladder we report on

Three separate profiles. **Never one combined level** — this is a reading, writing and speaking
instrument, and a learner is routinely two steps higher in speaking than in writing.

| Band | Reading | Writing | Speaking |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pre-A1.1** | Recognises some letters; recognises own name in print | Marks the page; traces or copies letters | Says own name; single words |
| **Pre-A1.2** | Reads own name and a few taught whole words | Writes own name unaided | Answers "What is your name / Where are you from?" |
| **Pre-A1.3** | Sounds out short taught words (*sun*, *hat*, *bag*); matches word to picture | Copies a short frame accurately; fills one slot | Uses 2–3 taught frames with support |
| **A1.1** | Reads familiar words and very short phrases | Completes a personal-details form | Introduces self in short simple sentences |
| **A1.2** | Reads a very simple sentence about a familiar topic | Writes 3–5 true simple sentences | Introduces self and answers simple questions |
| **A2.1** | Finds specific information in a short everyday text | Writes a short note or message | Simple exchange on familiar routine matters |
| **A2.2** | Reads a short simple personal text and gets the gist | Writes a very simple personal letter | Describes background and immediate environment |
| **B1** | Understands mainly high-frequency everyday text | Writes simple connected text | Deals with familiar matters; gives brief reasons |
| **Above range** | _Tops out the B1 booklet — refer for separate placement, do not grade here_ | | |

**What is ours and what is the CEFR's.** Only the whole levels — Pre-A1, A1, A2, B1 — are CEFR
levels. Pre-A1 comes from the CEFR Companion Volume; the three steps inside it are ours,
LASLLIAM-shaped. The halves (A1.1/A1.2, A2.1/A2.2) are also ours: the CEFR does not define them.
The convention is a common one — course providers routinely split a level into halves — and we need
it here because **a 50-hour course moves a learner about half a CEFR level**. On a whole-level scale
almost every learner would start A1 and finish A1, and the endline would show nothing. A2.2 is
roughly what the CEFR itself calls A2+, the one plus-level in our range.

The rule that keeps the halves honest: **each is defined by the observable descriptors in the table
above, not by "half the marks of A1"**. A sub-band derived from a score cut alone is fake precision.
Reports carry both — the whole CEFR level, and the sub-band underneath it.

From A1 up, the descriptors are lifted close to the wording of the CEFR self-assessment grid on
purpose, so the levels we report are the levels a partner can look up.

## The instrument

### Part 0 — Foundations (8 min, one-to-one, orally administered)

For this cohort this is not a screener. **It is the main measurement zone**, and it is where most
learners' gains will appear.

Six short tasks, each scored as a count, not a judgement: point to letters named aloud · find own
name among four names · read five taught whole words · match four words to four pictures · copy a
short phrase · write own name. Administered warmly, one to one, with an explicit "we can stop at
any point" and no correction during the task.

Score routes the learner: below the Pre-A1.3 threshold, they **stop here** and are reported on the
Pre-A1 steps. Nobody sits twenty minutes in front of a paper they cannot read.

### Part 1 — Core (12 min, whole group, ~20 items)

Everyone above the Pre-A1.3 threshold. Targeted across A1.1–A2.2 with three B1 items at the end as a
routing signal. Item types kept guess-resistant: word–picture matching (five options for three
gaps), signs and notices, short gap-fill from a word box, a 70-word text with short-answer
questions. No more than 40% of the marks from four-option multiple choice.

### Part 2A — Lower booklet (18 min) · A1–A2

Reading: an SMS from a friend, a health-post opening-times notice, a learning-centre timetable —
find specific predictable information, which is the A2 reading descriptor almost verbatim.
Writing: complete a personal-details registration form (the A1 writing descriptor), write five true
sentences about yourself, write a short note to a friend (~40 words).

### Part 2B — Reach booklet (20 min) · B1

Only for learners who clear the routing threshold. A 180-word text with inference and
vocabulary-in-context items, and a 100–120-word personal letter describing an experience — the B1
reading and writing descriptors, and nothing above them. Kept deliberately short: for this cohort
it will be used rarely, and its job is to prevent a ceiling, not to grade an intermediate
population. A learner who clears it comfortably is marked *above range*, not promoted to a band
this instrument does not carry.

### Part 3 — Oral check (6 min, one-to-one)

Included because the component is oral-and-visual-first and puts speaking before writing. A
reading-and-writing-only endline would show near-zero gain for exactly the learners who gained
most.

Same-gender pairing where it matters. The facilitator follows a script; the learner may pass on any
prompt. Nothing is recorded unless the learner agrees.

| | Prompt | Targets | Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Warm-up** _(unscored)_ | "Hello. My name is ___. What is your name?" | settles the learner | 30s |
| **2. Personal information** | Name · age or how long here · where you are from · who you live with. Ask, wait, and re-ask once in simpler words if needed. | Pre-A1.2 → A1.2 | 90s |
| **3. Picture prompt** | A line drawing of an everyday camp scene (the learning centre, a shop, collecting water). "What can you see? Tell me about this picture." | A1.1 → A2.1 | 90s |
| **4. Simple questions** | Three of: What do you do in the morning? · What do you like? · Who is your friend, and why? · What is the weather like today? | A1.2 → A2.2 | 90s |
| **5. Extended turn** _(only if 4 went easily)_ | "Tell me about something you want to do after this course." Follow up once with "Why?" | A2.2 → B1 | 60s |
| **6. Close** _(unscored)_ | "Thank you. You spoke English with me today." | ends warmly, always | 15s |

Scored on four observable strands, each on the Pre-A1.1–B1 ladder: **intelligibility** (can a listener
who does not know the learner follow it), **range** (single words → frames → own sentences),
**interaction** (answers the question actually asked; asks for repetition), **extended turn**
(strings two or more sentences together). Confidence and willingness are **recorded but not scored**
as language — the course explicitly targets them, and a learner who moves from silence to speaking
has moved, even if the words are the same.

## When there is no time for the one-to-ones

Parts 0 and 3 are one-to-one: about 14 minutes per learner, twice per course. For a class of 25
that is roughly six hours per sitting. That may not exist in the timetable, so both parts are
**optional** — but they are not equally droppable, and dropping them is not free.

### Three modes

| Mode | 1:1 time, class of 25 | What you get |
|---|---|---|
| **Standard** _(the default)_ | ~1 hour per sitting, **spread over five sessions** | Part 0 in group mode; oral kept 1:1 but sampled. Nearly all the data at a sixth of the cost. |
| **Full** | 5+ hours per sitting | Everything, per learner. Needs a second adult or a class under 12. |
| **Paper only** | none | Reading and writing only, from Part 1 upward. Cheapest, and the weakest claim. |

### One-to-one time is not just facilitator time — it is unsupervised-classroom time

The cost that matters is not the eight minutes with one learner; it is the twenty-four learners in
the room without a facilitator while it happens.

The obvious fix — spreading the one-to-ones across ordinary sessions — **does not work here**. The
baseline has to precede any teaching and the endline has to fall in the final week; a learner
assessed in the fifth session has already had five sessions of English, and at the bottom of the
ladder, where gains come fastest, that contamination is large. Baseline and endline are each **one
sitting**.

What makes a single sitting workable is that **on those two days nobody is missing teaching**, so a
learner who has finished can go home. The day runs: paper parts with the whole class together
(~40 minutes, facilitator leading, no supervision problem), then everyone except the oral sample
leaves, then the sample is seen **by staggered appointment** — 10:00, 10:08, 10:16. Ten learners
told to wait is a supervision problem; ten learners each given a time is not.

Where the group must arrive and leave together, the waiting learners get a holding activity — the
same picture-word and drawing pages, never frame practice, which is the language the talk measures.

Better still where it is possible: **do the baseline orals at registration**, one learner at a time
as they enrol. There is no class to supervise yet, and no teaching has happened. It is the cleanest
baseline available.

**This is also why group mode is the default rather than the fallback.** Part 0 one-to-one buys
slightly better information — reading aloud instead of circling — and costs over three hours. The
oral is worth its hour because it has no group substitute at all and measures the thing this
component is built to move.

### If the oral is sampled

Take **at least 10 learners, or 40% of the class, whichever is larger**. Choose them **at random
before the baseline**, and assess the *same* learners at endline. Random matters: a facilitator
choosing who to assess will choose the confident speakers, and the endline will look wonderful.

Sampling gives a defensible programme-level number — "learners gained on average X" — but no
speaking profile for the learners outside the sample.

### What dropping the oral actually costs

This component is built oral-and-visual-first. It "leads with the ear and the voice and treats
reading and writing as something that grows out of speaking, not a gate in front of it", and its own
assessment note points at the spoken self-introduction as primary evidence. Speaking is very
probably where Learning Bridge+ produces its largest gain.

A paper-only endline does not measure that gain. For the lowest learners — the ones the component
is designed around, who may move from silence to a spoken self-introduction while their writing
barely moves — it will report close to nothing. The programme will look weaker than it is, in a
document that goes to NRC.

If only one one-to-one part survives, **keep the oral and let Part 0 go to group mode.**

### The floor safeguard, in every mode

Paper-only mode removes the gate that stops a learner sitting twelve minutes in front of a paper
they cannot read — and removes the floor, so a non-reader scoring 3 on Part 1 would be converted to
Pre-A1.3 when they may be Pre-A1.1.

So in paper-only mode, the facilitator runs a **two-minute start check**: everyone attempts the
worked example together, and any learner who cannot begin it is quietly given the group-mode Part 0
sheet instead of Part 1. It costs two minutes for the whole class and prevents both the distress and
the false band.

## Marking

Marker English is unknown and will vary, so the **default mark scheme is a checklist**: countable,
observable features rather than impressionistic bands. For the "five true sentences" task, for
example, the marker ticks what is present — *five sentences attempted · each begins with a capital ·
each has a verb · uses "I am" or "I like" correctly · three or more are true and about the learner ·
a reader can understand all five without help* — and the tick count maps to a band on a printed
conversion table. Two markers with modest English land on the same band; that is the entire point.

An **analytic rubric** (task fulfilment, range, accuracy, coherence, with CEFR-derived band wording)
is built as an optional second route for markers who have been calibrated. Both routes map to the
same published ladder, so results stay comparable.

Either route ships with **benchmark scripts**: anonymised exemplar answers at each band that the
marker holds beside the learner's page. These do more for offline reliability than any amount of
rubric prose.

## Two forms that mean the same thing

Form A and Form B share one blueprint — same item count, same item types in the same order, same
target band per item slot, same topic domains. Only the content differs.

- **Topic domains do not vary between forms.** Otherwise a gain cannot be told apart from a lucky
  topic.
- **Six anchor items are identical across both forms**, to detect drift.
- **A gain is reported only when it clears a stated threshold** — one sub-band, or ≥4 raw marks on
  Part 1 — decided before the data arrives, not after.
- **Conditions must match**: same time allowance, same oral administration, same permitted support
  at both sittings. Written into the administration guide as a rule, not a suggestion.

## Context and its limits

Content is drawn from: block and shelter life, water and latrines, rations and distribution, the
health post and referral, the learning centre and timetable, monsoon and cyclone warnings and the
hills, tailoring and volunteering and small shops, family and neighbours, training and registration
forms.

**Excluded, without exception:** repatriation, violence, fire, loss of family, boat journeys, camp
politics, grievances about aid. The unit plan already commits that every activity "lets a learner
choose what to share, offers a lighter everyday focus, and never requires anything personal" — the
assessment must not be the one place that rule is dropped. Every writing and speaking prompt offers
a choice, and one option is always safely everyday. Every scenario must work for a young woman in a
same-gender group.

## Administration

Black-and-white photocopy-safe, no colour dependence, 14pt or larger, line drawings, generous white
space. All answers written **in the booklet** — no separate answer sheet, which is a known confound
for learners with low test literacy. An unscored worked example before every new task type.
Instructions may be given orally in Rohingya or Chittagonian; **the items themselves may not be
translated**. No dictionaries or phones. Timings are generous: this is not a speed test.

## What gets built

| Output | Notes |

| Form A booklet · Form B booklet | Parts 0–3 in one booklet per learner |
| **Marking sheet** | One double-sided page per learner. Every tick box in marking order. The facilitator touches one sheet, not five. |
| **Conversion card** | One side of A4, laminated, one per marker. Every total → step number, in one place. |
| Marking pack | Keys, benchmark scripts, and the optional analytic rubric for calibrated markers |
| Administration guide | `ENGLISH-ASSESSMENT-ADMIN-GUIDE.md` — written for a facilitator who does not know the CEFR |
| Class record sheet | Names, mode, and six step numbers. Gain is end minus start. |
| Learner profile sheet | One page of "I can now…" statements, mirroring `efi-mv-can-do-checklist` |
| Picture sheets | The line drawings named in the forms, black and white, photocopy-safe |

Content lives as `resource` materials (there is no `assessment` type in `lib/schema.ts`), at
`access: partner` — the answer keys must not be public. Downloads come from a new
`scripts/downloads/generate-english-assessment.js`, following the `generate-lb-guides.js` pattern.

## Build sequence

1. This plan, agreed.
2. **The blueprint** — item-by-item table (slot, type, target band, domain, marks). Source of truth
   for both forms; nothing gets written until it is fixed.
3. Form A, keys, checklist schemes, benchmark scripts.
4. Form B against the same blueprint, with a mechanical parallel check.
5. Administration guide, record sheets, learner profile.
6. Materials, site pages, generator, docx pack.
7. **A small trial** — 10–15 learners, two markers on the same eight scripts. Checks the floor, the
   routing thresholds, and marker agreement. Without it the improvement claim rests on assertion.

## Open questions

1. **Who administers Parts 0 and 3.** They are one-to-one and take ~14 minutes per learner. For a
   class of 25 that is roughly six hours of facilitator time per sitting, twice per course. Real,
   and needs planning into the timetable — or a second adult in the room.
2. **Does the endline double as evidence for anything else?** English is not one of the two graded
   Learning Bridge+ competencies, so this stays formative and informative by design. Confirm nobody
   downstream is treating it as a gate.
3. **Test security across cohorts.** Forms will circulate. Accepted, and the reason for anchor items
   and the partner access level — but worth a decision on whether a Form C is eventually needed.
