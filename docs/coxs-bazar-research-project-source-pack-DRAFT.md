# Research Project (Cox's Bazar) — Secondary Source Pack · DRAFT

Working draft for review. Offline artefact (USB/print) for the Learning Bridge (Cox's Bazar)
Research Project component. Shared challenge: **what is happening to the trees on the hills where we
live**. Research question (given): *"What happens to our hills without trees, and what changes when
trees and plants grow back?"*

## How this pack works
- **Every source has three reading levels:** the **original article** (real, authentic English — for
  teachers and the strongest readers, and to show learners we simplified from something real), a
  **B1 version**, and an **A1/A2 version**. Most learners read the B1 or A1/A2 version; facilitators
  read aloud for learners still building fluency.
- **Each source carries its own reference** (with the web address) — offline you can't click it, but
  citing the source at the point of use is good practice and is what we teach.
- **Trust signals are printed on every card** (a CRAAP test: *Who? When? Why? Do they show how they
  know? Does it help our question?*), because offline learners can't look anything up — so the pack
  supplies what they need to judge it. The offline "check it another way" move is **triangulation**:
  does this source agree with the *other* sources, and with our *own* primary research on the hills?
- **Deliberately mixed credibility:** the pack includes strong sources *and* a weak one, so evaluating
  sources (course objective 3) has real teeth.
- **Every glossed word feeds the programme word bank** (at the end of this pack) — the durable form of
  the classroom "research word wall."
- **Convention:** an organisation's full name is given first, then its short form — e.g. *Food and
  Agriculture Organization (FAO)*, then *FAO*.

## Copyright position (decided per source)
- **A — The Conversation:** CC BY-ND 4.0 → the whole original may be reproduced **with attribution**;
  our B1/A2 texts are our **own summaries of the facts** (facts are not copyright), not edits of their
  wording. *Reproduce the original verbatim from the page at production, with the site's attribution
  line — not re-typed here.*
- **B — FAO:** page shows "© FAO 2026"; FAO's standard terms permit **educational, non-commercial
  reproduction with full attribution** → reproduced below. (Confirm on the master list.)
- **C — UN in Bangladesh:** UN content, reproducible for **educational/non-commercial use with
  attribution** → reproduced below.
- **D — Grow Billion Trees:** commercial, all rights reserved → **not reproduced.** Cited and
  summarised only. (Fittingly, the least trustworthy source is also the one we can't copy.)

# Source A — Trees keep the land cool
*Sub-question 3 (heat and shade) · strong source · B1 stretch*

### ① Original article
*Reproduce verbatim from the source under CC BY-ND 4.0, with The Conversation's required attribution
line. Not re-typed here (BY-ND forbids altered copies, so we keep the original exact and write our own
summaries separately).*

**Reference:** Thompson, S., Corrêa, D., Duncan, J. & Crompton, O. "Deforestation can raise local
temperatures by up to 4.5°C – and heat untouched areas 6km away." *The Conversation*, 15 November 2021.
https://theconversation.com/deforestation-can-raise-local-temperatures-by-up-to-4-5-and-heat-untouched-areas-6km-away-163584

### ② B1 version
> Trees help keep the land cool, and cutting them down makes a place hotter. A tree pulls water up from
> the soil, through its body, and out of its leaves into the air. When the water changes into **vapour**,
> it carries heat away with it — the same way your skin feels cold when you step out of the water. This
> is called **evaporation**. Scientists at a university in Australia say that one tree in a hot country
> can cool the air around it about as much as two air-coolers running all day.
>
> When many trees are cut down, this cooling stops and the land heats up. The scientists used pictures
> from **satellites** — cameras high above the Earth — to measure the heat. They found that cutting the
> forest can make a place up to **4.5 degrees Celsius (°C)** hotter. The heat even reaches nearby forest
> that was not cut down, up to 6 kilometres away. Hotter land is also harder for farming: for every 1°C
> hotter, farmers grow about 3 to 7 **percent** less food.

### ③ A1/A2 version
> Trees keep the land cool. A tree takes water from the ground and lets it out through its leaves into
> the air. This makes the air cooler. When people cut the trees, the land gets hot. Scientists say a
> place with no trees can be much hotter — up to 4.5 degrees hotter. Hot land is also hard for growing
> food.

**🖼 Picture:** a tree with arrows (water up from the soil → vapour out of the leaves), a cool shape
around it; beside it, bare ground with heat lines rising.

**Trust signals (CRAAP):** **Who?** four named university scientists. **When?** 2021 (the science does
not go out of date). **Why?** to explain their research to ordinary people. **Show how they know?** yes
— a study other scientists checked; they even say who paid for the work. **Check another way:** does it
match the other cards and our own hills?

### ④ Glossary for this source *(→ programme word bank)*
- **vapour:** water that has turned into a gas in the air.
- **evaporation:** when water changes into vapour and rises into the air.
- **satellite:** a machine high above the Earth that can take pictures of the ground.
- **degree Celsius (°C):** the unit we use to measure how hot or cold something is.
- **percent (%):** a part of one hundred. "7 percent less" means 7 less out of every 100.
- **scientist:** a person who studies the world carefully to find out how it works.

# Source B — Making the bare hills safe again
*Sub-question 1 & 2 (before/now; a bare hill in the rain) · local action · strong, with self-interest · B1*

### ① Original article
*Reproduced under FAO's educational / non-commercial permission, with attribution. © FAO 2026.*

**Reference:** Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO), Regional Office for Asia
and the Pacific. "Protecting 45,000 Rohingya Lives Ahead of Life-Threatening Landslides." 28 April 2026.
https://www.fao.org/asiapacific/news/news-detail/protecting-45-000-rohingya-lives-ahead-of-life-threatening-landslides/en

> The Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO) in Bangladesh has received USD
> 584,369 from the Bangladesh Humanitarian Fund to implement urgent slope stabilization and community
> preparedness initiatives. These efforts aim to protect vulnerable Rohingya households from landslide
> risks before the approaching monsoon season.
>
> The funding will support stabilization work across approximately 170 hectares of high-risk slopes at
> 85 sites within 15 Rohingya refugee camps in Ukhia and Teknaf. The intervention strategy includes
> planting deep-rooted native vegetation, deploying bioengineering techniques such as bamboo crib walls
> and contour trenching, and offering emergency Cash-for-Work opportunities benefiting 800 direct
> participants. These measures are designed to protect over 45,000 Rohingya refugees by safeguarding
> shelters, evacuation routes, and access to essential services during monsoon months.
>
> FAO Representative Jiaoqun Shi emphasized the urgency: "Fragile and deforested slopes combined with
> extreme monsoon rainfall are increasing the risk of life-threatening landslides." He noted that
> sustainable prevention measures are critically needed to prevent loss of life and secondary
> displacement, and praised the Bangladesh Humanitarian Fund for addressing a significant gap in the
> 2025–2026 Rohingya Joint Response Plan.
>
> Shi highlighted that "nature-based solutions … deliver between USD 7 and USD 30 in returns for every
> USD 1 invested" while simultaneously reducing disaster risks and generating income.
>
> FAO brings substantial technical expertise in nature-based bioengineering solutions. Through the Safe
> Access to Fuel and Energy Plus (SAFE+) programme, FAO has already stabilized over 3,500 hectares of
> degraded slopes since 2018. The organization maintains a strong field presence in Cox's Bazar since
> the 2017 Rohingya influx, coordinating through the Rohingya Coordination Platform to align
> interventions with site management, shelter, and protection responses.
>
> Project activities will be delivered through local community structures including *majhis* (community
> leaders), *imams* (spiritual leaders), and youth networks to foster long-term ownership and
> self-reliance. [Remaining paragraphs on the Bangladesh Humanitarian Fund and FAO's global appeal
> reproduced in full in the built pack.]

### ② B1 version
> On the hills where the camps stand, the forest was cut down to make room for people, and the hills
> became bare. When the heavy **monsoon** rain falls, the soil slides down the bare **slopes**. These
> **landslides** are dangerous — they can bury shelters, block escape routes, and hurt people.
>
> The **Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO)**, a part of the United Nations, is working to make the
> hills safe again and to protect more than 45,000 people before the monsoon comes. The workers use
> three main methods. They plant **native** grasses and small trees with deep, strong **roots** that
> hold the soil like ropes under the ground. They build low walls of **bamboo** across the slope, called
> **crib walls**. And they dig long lines across the hill, called **contour trenches**, to slow the rain
> water so it does not rush straight down.
>
> In 2025 and 2026 this work was done on about 170 **hectares**, at 85 places in 15 camps. Since 2018,
> FAO says it has made more than 3,500 hectares of slope safer. About 800 people from the camps do the
> work and are paid for it through **Cash-for-Work**, so it protects the hills and gives families money
> at the same time.

### ③ A1/A2 version
> The hills in the camp have no trees now. When the big rain comes, the ground slips down the hill. This
> is a **landslide**. It can hurt people and break homes.
>
> A group called the **Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO)** wants to make the hills safe. Workers
> plant grass and small trees. The **roots** hold the ground. They build small walls of **bamboo** on
> the hill. People from the camp do this work. They get money for it.

**🖼 Picture:** a bare hillside with bamboo crib walls and trenches across it, people planting; arrows
show rain water being slowed by the walls.

**Trust signals (CRAAP):** **Who?** a United Nations agency — experts. **When?** 2026 — current. **Why?**
it reports FAO's *own* work, so it shows the work in a good light. **Show how they know?** yes — specific
numbers and a named spokesperson. **Check another way:** does it agree with the other cards and with
what *we* saw on our hills?

### ④ Glossary for this source *(→ programme word bank)*
- **Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO):** a part of the United Nations that helps countries with
  food, farming, and land.
- **monsoon:** the season of very heavy rain.
- **landslide:** when soil and rock slide down a hill.
- **slope:** the sloping side of a hill.
- **native plants:** plants that grow naturally in a place.
- **roots:** the part of a plant that grows under the ground and holds it in place.
- **bamboo:** a tall, strong plant with hard, hollow stems.
- **crib wall:** a low wall, here made of bamboo, built across a slope to hold the soil.
- **contour trench:** a long, shallow ditch dug across a hill to slow rain water.
- **hectare:** a unit for measuring land — about the size of a large sports field (10,000 m²).
- **Cash-for-Work:** paid work given to community members on a local project.

# Source C — Half a million trees
*Sub-question 1 (before/now) · local action · the "currency" trap · A2*

### ① Original article
*Reproduced under UN educational / non-commercial permission, with attribution. © United Nations in
Bangladesh.*

**Reference:** Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO) / United Nations in
Bangladesh. "Nearly half a million trees planted in two months: FAO restores degraded watersheds and
forests in Cox's Bazar." 7 January 2020.
https://bangladesh.un.org/en/30306-nearly-half-million-trees-planted-two-months-fao-restores-degraded-watersheds-and-forests-cox

> The Food and Agriculture Organization completed a two-month reforestation initiative in watershed
> areas near Rohingya camps. Through the SAFE Plus project — a collaboration between FAO, IOM and WFP —
> nearly half a million tree seedlings were planted in partnership with Cox's Bazar South Forest
> Division.
>
> During October and November, local workers from disadvantaged communities planted 475,000 tree
> seedlings and removed unwanted vegetation across 571 hectares. Combined with 25,000 seedlings near
> camps and 500,000 inside camps, the total reached one million trees, replacing losses from settlement
> development and firewood demand.
>
> The project established 16 new local nurseries, with FAO supporting 45 total nurseries through
> training and assistance. This approach strengthened the local economy while creating sustainable tree
> sourcing.
>
> The Watershed and Forest Rehabilitation project, jointly funded by Japan, the Netherlands, Canada and
> IOM, targeted upland forest areas that capture rainwater for rivers and streams. These forests
> prevent landslides, maintain water quality, support biodiversity, and protect against natural
> hazards. [Remaining detail on planting distances and canal-bank planting reproduced in full in the
> built pack.]

### ② B1 version
> A few years ago, in 2020, a large tree-planting took place near the camps. In just two months, in
> October and November, workers planted 475,000 young trees — nearly half a million — on 571
> **hectares** of bare land. With more trees planted inside and around the camps, the total reached
> about one million trees. The planting was meant to bring back the forest that was cut down when the
> camps were built and when families cut wood to cook.
>
> The work was done by the **Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO)** together with two other United
> Nations groups, under a project called **SAFE Plus**. Most of the planting was done by local
> Bangladeshi workers from poor villages nearby — not by Rohingya **refugees**. The project also started
> 16 new tree **nurseries** — places where young trees are grown — to keep a supply of trees and to
> give local people work. The new forests sit on the high land that catches rain water for the rivers.
> Forests like these hold the soil, stop landslides, and keep the water clean.

### ③ A1/A2 version
> In 2020, workers planted many young trees near the camps. In two months they planted about half a
> million trees on the bare land. They wanted to bring back the **forest** that was cut down. Local
> people from villages near the camps did most of the planting. Trees hold the soil, stop
> **landslides**, and keep the water clean.

**🖼 Picture:** rows of small tree seedlings covering a hillside; a calendar showing "2020".

**Trust signals (CRAAP):** **Who?** the UN / FAO — experts. **When?** **2020 — old** (five+ years ago;
does the pack's Source B tell a newer story?). **Why?** to report the programme's work. **Show how they
know?** yes — clear numbers. **One thing to notice:** the planters were *local villagers, not
refugees* — easy to misread. **Check another way:** compare its date with Source B.

### ④ Glossary for this source *(→ programme word bank)*
- **hectare:** *(see Source B)* a unit for measuring land, about the size of a large sports field.
- **nursery:** a place where young plants and trees are grown until they are big enough to plant out.
- **refugee:** a person who has had to leave their own country to be safe.
- **Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO):** *(see Source B)*.
- **SAFE Plus:** the name of a United Nations project in the camps (its full name is Safe Access to Fuel
  and Energy Plus).
- **forest:** a large area of land covered with many trees.

# Source D — "Trees stop erosion" (a company's website)
*Sub-question 2 & 3 (a bare hill in the rain; what roots do) · weak source, on purpose · A2–B1*

### ① Original article
**Not reproduced.** This is a commercial company's website (all rights reserved), so we may read it and
cite it, but not copy it into the pack. That limit is itself worth noticing when we judge the source.

**Reference:** Grow Billion Trees (no named author). "How Can Trees Help Prevent Soil Erosion? Complete
Guide." growbilliontrees.com. https://growbilliontrees.com/blogs/knowledge/how-can-trees-help-prevent-soil-erosion

### ② B1 version
> A tree-planting **company** writes on its **website** that trees stop soil from being washed away.
> When rain falls on a bare hill, the water runs down fast and carries the soil with it. This is called
> **erosion**. The company says that tree **roots** hold the soil in place, and that the leaves of the
> trees slow the rain before it hits the ground, so less soil is lost. The website gives numbers — for
> example, that trees can cut soil loss by a large amount — but it does not say where these numbers come
> from, and no writer's name is given. The company sells a service: people pay it to plant trees.

### ③ A1/A2 version
> A **company** says that trees stop the soil from washing away. When rain falls on a bare hill, the
> water runs down fast and takes the soil with it. This is called **erosion**. Tree **roots** hold the
> soil. The company wants people to pay it to plant trees.

**🖼 Picture:** two hills side by side — one bare with brown water running off, one covered in plants
with clear ground below.

**Trust signals (CRAAP):** **Who?** a company — **no writer's name**. **When?** the date looks wrong (a
future date). **Why?** it **sells tree-planting**, so it wants trees to sound good. **Show how they
know?** no — numbers with no source. **Check another way:** the idea matches the stronger cards, but
this source shows no proof and is selling something → we trust it **least**.

### ④ Glossary for this source *(→ programme word bank)*
- **company:** a business that sells something to make money.
- **website:** a page on the internet.
- **erosion:** when soil is slowly worn away and carried off by water or wind.
- **roots:** *(see Source B)*.
